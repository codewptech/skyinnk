## Learning outcomes

By the end of this draft lesson, you should be able to:

- Distinguish the **format of a formal letter** from a formal email.
- Identify the sender, recipient, purpose, supporting details and requested action.
- Write a clear, polite formal letter based on a given situation, with a **100–120-word main response**.
- Revise a draft for relevance, tone, organisation, word count and accurate details.
- Avoid treating a request as an approval already granted.

## 1. Email and formal letter are not the same format

Both may use a respectful tone, clear subject, relevant details and an actionable request. However, a formal letter usually includes a **sender's address, date and recipient's address before the subject and salutation**, with a closing/signature after the message.

A formal email normally uses address/subject fields in the email interface. Copying an email body into a letter answer without adapting the format can omit important letter conventions.

In a classroom exercise, use **fictional or placeholder addresses**. You do not need to invent a real student's personal contact details to practise the format.

## 2. Plan before writing

Read this **fictional prompt**:

*Several streetlights near a community library in an imaginary town have stopped working. Students visit the library after school, and poorly lit roads make evening travel difficult. Write a formal letter to the municipal maintenance officer requesting an inspection and repairs.*

Before writing, note:

| Question | Plan for this letter |
| --- | --- |
| Who is writing? | A representative of students using the library |
| Who can act? | The municipal maintenance officer |
| What happened? | Streetlights near the library are not working |
| Why does it matter? | Evening visitors need safer visibility |
| What can reasonably be requested? | Inspection, repairs and an update |
| What is **not** known? | The repair date and whether permission or funding is already granted |

The letter should not claim the official has approved the request or promise that repairs will happen on a particular day.

## 3. Original model formal letter

*Every location and situation below is fictional. Bracketed address fields are placeholders rather than real personal information. The main message contains approximately 100–120 words; exact word-count conventions for addresses and closings require teacher confirmation.*

**From:**  
Student Reading Club Representative  
[School name, fictional town]  
[Sender's postal address]

**Date:** [Date of writing]

**To:**  
The Municipal Maintenance Officer  
[Fictional town municipality]  
[Office postal address]

**Subject: Request to repair streetlights near the community library**

Sir/Madam,

I am writing on behalf of students who visit the community library after school.

The recent rains have left several streetlights near the community library damaged. Students attending evening classes must walk along a poorly lit road, and residents have reported difficulty seeing approaching vehicles.

I request your office to inspect the lights and arrange repairs at the earliest. A temporary safety notice would also help pedestrians until the work is completed. The library is an important learning space, and a safer route would benefit students and other visitors.

Please share an estimated repair schedule and the contact point for further updates. Thank you for considering this request.

Yours faithfully,  
Student Reading Club Representative

### Why this works

1. The opening identifies the writer and purpose.
2. The middle paragraphs explain a **specific, fictional** problem and why action would help.
3. The request names **feasible next steps** rather than demanding an impossible promise.
4. The formal closing matches the salutation.
5. The sender, date and recipient details are present as deliberate **placeholders** for a practice exercise, without exposing a real learner's private information.

### What not to do

**Weak:** “The lights are bad. Fix them tomorrow. You have already agreed.”

**Improved:** “Please arrange an inspection and let us know the likely schedule for repairs.”

The improved version is clearer, respectful and does not falsely claim the municipality already approved the work.

## 4. A reliable writing sequence

**First:** read the prompt closely. Underline the **recipient, issue and requested action**.

**Second:** put the letter elements in a consistent order: sender's address → date → recipient's address → subject → salutation → message paragraphs → closing and name.

**Third:** draft the body in three sections:

- **Opening:** why you are writing.
- **Evidence and explanation:** relevant details the prompt supplies, without invented facts.
- **Action request:** a clear, courteous and realistic request.

**Fourth:** check the length. For practice, aim for a main message comfortably within **100–120 words**, while seeking teacher confirmation of what to count in your actual assessment. Prioritise meaning over filling space with repetitive words.

**Finally:** proofread for audience, tense, spelling, punctuation and consistency. Ask whether the reader could act on your request without guessing what you mean.

## Practice — write independently

1. **Letter to the editor:** In a fictional town, a public park lacks bins and visitors have been leaving litter near the walking paths. Write a formal letter to the editor of an imaginary local newspaper suggesting practical action. Use a respectful tone and a 100–120-word body. Do not allege wrongdoing by identifiable real people.
2. **Letter of request:** A fictional school reading club wants permission to hold an after-school reading session in a shared hall. Write a formal letter to the school activities coordinator explaining who will attend, what is planned, how the hall will be used responsibly and what permission is requested. Do **not** imply the venue is already booked.
3. Rewrite this sentence so that it makes a **request**, not a claim of approval: “The municipality has agreed to replace every light tomorrow, so tell the workers to come.”
4. Put these elements in order: closing, recipient's address, date, subject, sender's address, body, salutation.
5. Review your response to Task 1. Circle one claim based directly on the prompt and underline one useful request. Delete any detail you added without evidence.

## Sample guidance and self-check

**Task 1:** A strong response should state the problem without exaggeration, explain how litter affects the shared park, and request reasonable steps such as better disposal facilities, signs or a community clean-up. Different valid letters are possible; the review criteria below matter more than memorising one fixed answer.

**Task 2:** Identify the club, event and proposed arrangements, then request confirmation of availability or another suitable time. If an exact time is unknown, do not invent a confirmed booking. The separate *formal-email* lesson demonstrates similar content, but your answer here must follow **letter** layout.

**Task 3 — possible correction:** “Please let us know whether the streetlights can be inspected and when repair work might be scheduled.”

**Task 4 — correct order:** sender's address → date → recipient's address → subject → salutation → body → closing.

**Task 5:** There is no single correct sentence to circle or underline. The **issue** should be supported by the fictional prompt, and the **request** should be relevant and achievable.

### Independent checklist

- Does my response follow the formal-letter layout, **not** the email format?
- Is the recipient appropriately addressed?
- Does the subject tell the reader why I am writing?
- Are the facts from the prompt used accurately?
- Are my requests clear, courteous and reasonable?
- Is the main response within the teacher-confirmed word limit?
- Have I checked grammar and avoided disclosing personal information?
- Have I kept fictional examples distinct from real events and unverified assertions?
