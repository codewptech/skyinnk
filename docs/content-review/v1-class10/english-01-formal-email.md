## Learning outcomes

After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Identify the purpose, audience and expected response of a formal email.
- Write a meaningful subject line and respectful greeting.
- Organise a request into a clear opening, relevant details and a specific next step.
- Use concise, polite language without sounding demanding or overly familiar.
- Proofread a draft for completeness, grammar and tone.

## 1. Why do we write formal emails?

A formal email is a written message to someone with whom we have an official or professional relationship, such as a school coordinator, teacher, librarian or organisation. You might use one to request permission, ask for information, report a problem or respond to an invitation.

A good email should allow the reader to answer three questions quickly:

1. **Why are you writing?** State the purpose early.
2. **What information does the reader need?** Include the essential facts, not an entire story.
3. **What action or reply are you requesting?** End with a clear, polite next step.

A formal tone is respectful and direct. It does **not** require complicated vocabulary or long sentences.

## 2. Plan before you type

Imagine a fictional school reading club wishes to use the library hall for a session. Complete a five-part plan:

| Part | Planning question | Example detail |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Recipient | Who can decide? | Activities Coordinator |
| Purpose | What do we want? | Permission to use the library hall |
| Occasion | What is planned? | A 40-minute reading session |
| Practical details | What will be needed? | Approximately 20 students and chairs |
| Request | What should the recipient do? | Confirm a suitable day or suggest another slot |

Only include a specific date if it is known and accurate. If scheduling is undecided, state the proposed time and ask for confirmation rather than presenting it as an approved booking.

## 3. A complete model email

This is an **original fictional example**, not a message that has actually been sent.

**Subject:** Request to Use the Library Hall for a Reading Session

Dear Activities Coordinator,

I am writing on behalf of our Class 10 reading club to request permission to hold a 40-minute reading session in the library hall next Thursday after school.

We expect around twenty students to participate. The session will include short readings from original student writing and a brief discussion. We will bring our materials, arrange the chairs with staff guidance and leave the hall tidy afterwards.

Please let us know whether the hall is available at that time or whether another slot would be more suitable.

Thank you for considering our request.

Yours sincerely,  
A Class 10 Reading Club Representative

### Why this example works

- The **subject** tells the reader the main request.
- The **first paragraph** identifies who is writing and what permission is needed.
- The **middle paragraph** adds numbers, activity, duration and responsibilities.
- The **last paragraph** asks for a decision without assuming approval.
- The **closing** matches the respectful greeting.

The signature is fictional and deliberately does not include a real student’s email address, telephone number or other personal information. In an actual school submission, use only the identity and contact details authorised by the school.

## 4. Make the tone appropriately formal

Compare these pairs:

| Too casual or unclear | More appropriate |
| --- | --- |
| “Hey! Need the hall ASAP.” | “I am writing to request permission to use the hall.” |
| “You must give us Thursday.” | “Please let us know whether Thursday would be suitable.” |
| “Stuff is happening there.” | “We plan to conduct a 40-minute reading session.” |
| “Send a reply now.” | “I would appreciate your response when convenient.” |

Avoid unnecessary phrases such as “I humbly beg to state” or several sentences that repeat the same request. Clear language is usually stronger than exaggerated politeness.

## 5. Build and revise a draft

A useful structure for a school request is:

**Subject** — a short phrase naming the action requested.

**Greeting** — “Dear Activities Coordinator,” or another appropriate title.

**Opening** — who you are and why you are writing.

**Details** — the event, proposed timing, people involved and any practical needs.

**Action request** — what approval, clarification or reply you need.

**Closing** — thanks and a respectful sign-off.

### Editing exercise

Improve the fictional message below without changing what the student actually knows:

> Hi sir. We want to do a reading thing. Give us the library next week. Around 20 people are coming. Tell us fast. Thanks.

Notice the missing purpose, vague timing, demanding tone and absent detail about what participants will do. An improved email should clarify the request **without inventing a confirmed date, permission or staff agreement**.

## Practice: write independently

1. Write a formal email of approximately **100–120 words** requesting permission to organise a class reading session. Include a clear subject, audience, approximate number of participants, planned activity and a polite request for confirmation. Treat every detail as part of a fictional exercise.
2. Rewrite this sentence in a respectful, clear way: “We have to use the auditorium tomorrow, so arrange it.”
3. Choose the more useful subject line and explain why:
   - (a) “Important”
   - (b) “Request for Permission to Hold a Reading Session”
4. Your assignment may be delayed because of an unexpected family commitment. Draft a **70–90 word** email asking your teacher whether a short extension is possible. Explain the situation briefly, propose a realistic new date and ask respectfully; do not presume the extension is approved.
5. Read your email aloud. Remove two unnecessary words or repeated ideas without losing an important fact.

## Sample responses and self-check

**Task 1 — possible response:** Use the model email above as a guide, but change the wording and details yourself. A good answer must include the request, purpose, practical information and next step, rather than merely copying the model.

**Task 2 — improved sentence:** “I am writing to ask whether the auditorium might be available tomorrow. Please let us know if the proposed time is possible.”

**Task 3:** (b) is stronger because it names the purpose. The recipient can understand the request before opening the message.

**Task 4 — possible opening and closing:**

> **Subject:** Request for a Short Assignment Extension
>
> Dear Teacher,
>
> I am writing to ask whether I may submit the assignment two days after the original deadline. An unexpected family commitment has reduced the time available for completing the work. I have finished the planning and will use the additional time to review the final answer carefully.
>
> Please let me know if the revised submission date would be acceptable. I understand that the original deadline remains in place unless you approve a change.
>
> Thank you for considering my request.
>
> Yours sincerely,  
> A Class 10 Student

This is an illustrative answer; your teacher may expect a different format or amount of detail.

### Final editing checklist

Before sending a real message, confirm:

- Is the recipient correct?
- Does the subject identify the request?
- Is the opening purpose clear?
- Are essential details accurate, not guessed?
- Is the requested next step specific and polite?
- Are spelling, punctuation, paragraph breaks and closing appropriate?
- Have you avoided sharing unnecessary private information?

## Before moving on

Write one sentence describing the difference between **asking for approval** and **claiming that approval has already been granted**. Formal writing should make that difference clear.
