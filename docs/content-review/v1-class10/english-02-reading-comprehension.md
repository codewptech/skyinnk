After this lesson, you should be able to:

- Identify information stated directly in a passage.
- Make a reasonable inference and support it with evidence from the text.
- Distinguish an observation from an assumption or a claim about cause.
- Understand selected words from context rather than guessing.
- Write concise answers in your own words without adding unsupported facts.

## 1. Read with a purpose

A reading-comprehension question usually asks one of three things:

1. **Literal detail:** What does the author actually say?
2. **Inference:** What can you reasonably conclude from what the author says?
3. **Evaluation:** Is a claim fully supported by the evidence, or is something missing?

You can use a simple method: **Question → Evidence → Answer**. First identify exactly what the question asks. Next find the relevant sentence or paragraph. Finally answer in your own clear words, using only information the passage justifies.

Read the following original fictional passage twice. On the first reading, try to understand the situation. On the second, underline details that could support an answer.

## 2. Original passage: The Garden Notebook

**Paragraph 1**

At the fictional Hillview School, an environmental club cared for six small vegetable beds near the courtyard. After several rainy days, the soil beside the wall still looked dry. A large tank stood near the garden to collect rainwater from a nearby roof. Some students assumed that the tank would be full after so much rain. When they checked its level window, however, they saw that very little water had collected.

**Paragraph 2**

Two students traced the path that rainwater should follow from the roof to the tank. From the ground, they noticed a thick layer of leaves around a gutter opening. They told the school caretaker, who inspected the gutter and safely removed the blockage. The students did not climb onto the roof or try to repair the gutter themselves. Instead, they asked how they could help from the ground and agreed to observe the system more carefully.

**Paragraph 3**

The club began a simple notebook. Each school day, they recorded whether it had rained, the level shown on the tank, and how many watering cans they had carried from the tap. During the first five days, they carried twelve cans from the tap. During the next five days, they carried seven. The club was pleased, but it did not claim that clearing the leaves **alone** had caused the decrease. The amount of rain had changed between the two periods, and the students had also adjusted their watering schedule.

**Paragraph 4**

At the next school assembly, the club shared its observations and explained what it still did not know. One student said, “A notebook is useful because it reminds us to report what we actually saw, not just what we expected.” The club decided to continue recording results before making a stronger claim. Their small project showed that curiosity becomes more useful when it is joined with careful evidence and cooperation.

## 3. Find evidence before forming an answer

Consider this question:

**Why did the students check the tank?**

A useful answer is: *They checked it because the garden soil still looked dry after rain, even though they expected rainwater to have collected in the tank.*

The answer combines two details from paragraph 1. It does not invent a broken pipe or an empty underground supply; the passage never mentions either.

Now consider:

**What can we infer about the club’s attitude toward evidence?**

A reasonable inference is: *The students wanted to be accurate rather than announce a result too quickly.* Paragraphs 3 and 4 support that inference because they recorded observations, noticed that rainfall and the watering schedule also changed, and avoided claiming one cause without enough evidence.

An inference goes a little beyond the literal words, but it must stay anchored to them.

## 4. Observation, assumption and explanation

| Type | Example from the passage | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | The tank level window showed little water. | A direct report of what students checked |
| Assumption | The tank would be full after rainy days. | A belief they had not yet tested |
| Possible explanation | Leaves blocking the gutter may have reduced the water reaching the tank. | Plausible, but further measurement is needed to establish how much difference it made |
| Unsupported conclusion | Clearing the leaves caused all of the reduction in tap-water use. | Other factors also changed |

The third row is a possible explanation, **not** a proven result. Good readers notice qualifying words such as *may*, *probably*, *alone*, *however* and *although*.

### A vocabulary clue

In paragraph 2, the students “traced the path” of the rainwater. Here, *traced* means **followed or examined the route**, not drew a picture of a person.

In paragraph 3, the word *adjusted* means **changed slightly for a purpose**. The surrounding sentence mentions a changed watering schedule, which helps you understand the word.

## Practice: answer from the passage

1. What detail in paragraph 1 surprised the students after the rainy days?
2. What did the students notice near the gutter opening?
3. Why did the students ask the caretaker for help instead of climbing onto the roof?
4. How many **fewer** watering cans did the club carry from the tap in the second five-day period?
5. Give **two** reasons the club could not confidently say that clearing the leaves alone caused the reduction.
6. Write one observation and one assumption from the passage. Label each.
7. In context, what does *traced* mean? Explain in a few words.
8. What is the main lesson of the passage? Write a one- or two-sentence summary using your own words.
9. A classmate says, “The tank definitely became full after the caretaker cleared the gutter.” Is that claim supported by the passage? Explain.
10. Suggest **one additional measurement** that might help the club compare water collection fairly across future days. Say why it would help.

## Answers with supporting evidence

1. The soil near the wall was still dry, and the tank contained very little water even after rain. These facts appear in paragraph 1.
2. They noticed a thick layer of leaves near a gutter opening (paragraph 2).
3. The passage does not state a direct quotation of their motive, but it strongly supports the inference that climbing or repairing the gutter was a staff safety task. The caretaker inspected and removed the blockage while the students stayed on the ground (paragraph 2).
4. **Five fewer cans**, because $12-7=5$ (paragraph 3). Do not confuse the decrease with the seven cans used in the second period.
5. Rainfall differed between the two periods, and the students had also changed their watering schedule (paragraph 3).
6. **Observation:** the level window showed little water. **Assumption:** the tank would be full after rainy days (paragraph 1).
7. It means they followed or examined the route the water should take (paragraph 2).
8. One possible summary: *Students investigating an unexpectedly dry school garden learned to collect observations and avoid claiming a cause without enough evidence. Cooperation and careful records helped them ask better questions.*
9. **No.** The passage says the students initially saw little water and later kept a record, but it never says the tank became full. “Definitely full” adds an unsupported detail.
10. One acceptable suggestion is to record **daily rainfall in a consistent unit**, so the club can compare water collected with the amount of rain that actually fell. Another reasonable measurement is the **change in tank water level before and after rain**, recorded at consistent times. Explain why the chosen measurement would reduce guesswork.

Some open questions may have more than one good answer. A strong answer must still match the evidence and explain its reasoning clearly.

## 5. A short independent writing challenge

Write **80–100 words** explaining whether careful observation makes teamwork more effective. Use at least two concrete details from the passage, but do not simply copy entire sentences.

Before finishing, ask yourself:

- Did I answer the actual question?
- Is each important claim supported by the passage?
- Did I separate what happened from what I only think might have happened?
- Could a reader follow my explanation without seeing my notes?

## Before moving on

The passage does not prove exactly how much water the cleared gutter saved. Explain why admitting that uncertainty makes the club’s report **more trustworthy**, not less.
